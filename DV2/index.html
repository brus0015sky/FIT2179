Learning and Memory in the Brain


As humans, our unique ability to learn new things and aquire new skills helped lead to our development from hunter gatherers to a complex and built-up society. Through our neural connections we can store a huge amount of information across a lifetime, and develop skills that require extremely precise muscle movements. Seeing that this is so important, we may want to figure out how we can quantify, evaluate and improve the brain's ability of neuroplasticity. This visualisation will explore that.

The first part of this visualisaion will look for various factors' influence on academic ability (where academic ability is approximating cognitive ability) and the second part will look at the physiological causes of disorders that impair cognitive ability.

Potential influences on cognitive ability

These graphs attempt to use Naplan scores among year 9 students as a proxy for cognitive abilities. The chart above looks for a possible correlation between more screen time and worse academic performance (Average Naplan score), but it appears the opposite may be true. This could be because in higher socioeconomic areas, people have more access to devices and also better education.

This chart looks for a correlaition between poor air quality (shown by CO2 emissions per area) and decreased academic performance, but again it appears the socioeconomic factors are much more significant. More built-up areas create more CO2 emissions and also have better access to education.

Influences continued

These charts attempt to find links between GPA and sleep, stress, and exercise respectively. It is from google form submissions from students of various universities. Sleep is often known to help with memory consolidation (especially REM sleep), but too much sleep would take a way from time spent studying. A very weak positive correlation is visible. Stress also has an influence on memory and learning.

Brief, moderate stress can sharpen focus and enhance the retention of immediate details, while chronic stress releases excess cortisol, impairing the brain's ability to encode and retrieve memories. Exercise can improve memory and learning by increasing blood flow to the brain, but in this case the time sacrifice of exercising many hours per day is likely a larger factor on GPA

What physiologically causes cognitive decline?

Here are PET brain scans of a dementia-afflicted patient vs a healthy patient. A brighter colour idicates a greater density of synapses (SUVR). A relatively darker area could indicate synaptic decay. This is a single slice taken around the hippocampus level in the brain.

Some effects of dementia are visible when comparing these two heatmaps. The Dementia afflicted patient appears to have some decline in the left hemisphere (right side of the heatmap), which could correlate with difficulties in language. Density of synapses is one way to detect for cognitive impairment, but what other ways are there?

Another measurement of brain activity

Instead of synapse density, these visualisaitions show the strength of different types of brain waves in different brain areas, captured by EEG devices. The T brain regions (temporal lobe) are most closely associated with memory formation and retreival, whereas the P regions (parietal lobe) are responsible for processing sensory informaiton. The Alzheimer's patient has a noticeable lack of alpha waves (8-12Hz). Alpha waves come from synchronised activity accross large groups of neurons,

and are known to be less present in Alzheimer's patients. the lack of alpha waves is associated with difficulty forming coherent memories, and fragmented cognition. Another significant difference is the overall decrease in higher frequency (beta and gamma) wave activity in the Alzheimer's patient. This is expected, as the neurodegeneration leads to the slowing of neural activity. For healthy patients, this is associated with being in deep sleep.

A closer look

Here is another look at the EEG data, this time including all measured brain regions. F refers to the Frontal lobe, whereas O refers to the Occipital lobe. The healthy patient has strong alpha and beta waves present in the Occipital lobe, missing in the Alzheimer's patient.

Since the occipital lobe is known to aid in visual processing and visual memory, lack of higher frequency waves here could indicate that this part of the brain is less focused and in a more sleep-like state. This is expected, as Alzheimer's disease often impacts people's depth perception and face recognitiion, among other things.
